# Practising arpeggios

Arpeggios contains four exercises: a study in a key, over the changes, Harris lines and Ted
Greene's exercises. The
description below explains examples printed by the app itself.

[← back to the README](../README.md)

---

## A few words first

An **arpeggio** is the notes of a chord played one after another instead of together. The C7
chord is C E G B♭, and its arpeggio is the same notes played as a line.

As in Intervals, the notes are written as **numbers counted from the root of the chord**. `3` is
the third of the chord on screen: in C7 that is E, in Cm7 E♭. In the tables each note name has its
number under it, so the numbers soon feel familiar.

You choose the exercise in the quick options of Arpeggios or in Settings, on the Practice tab.

## The set of exercises

The exercises fall into four groups: from the shape of an arpeggio on the neck, through the chords
of a tune, to lines and phrases. Each group builds on the one before. You do not have to finish one
to start the next: ten minutes of an earlier group is a good warm-up for the next. The settings in
bold are in the quick options of Arpeggios.

**Where to start.** With the first step of group 1: one chord, one key, two octaves up. Leave
**Captions** and **Chord context** on, play about half a second a note, and read the numbers, not
the note names. Each group says what it should leave in your hand and when to move on. That is a
hint, not a gate: you come back to the earlier groups.

### Group 1. The shape

One chord in one key (**Study in a key**). You learn where the chord's notes lie on the neck.
Move on when you play the arpeggio in a new key without stopping at the numbers.

1. **Two Octaves Up**, then **Two Octaves Down from the Root**. Play slowly and visualise every
   note on the neck.
2. **Groups of Four Up**, then **Down**. The same arpeggio cut into groups of four notes, each from
   the next chord tone: `1 3 5 7`, `3 5 7 1`, `5 7 1 3`… That is how Ted Greene practised, so the
   shape is not just one run from the bottom to the top.
3. **Triplets**, then the minor, major and dominant studies over their chords.
4. Turn **Random order** on: a new key after every pass.
5. **Ted Greene**, **The whole position**: the arpeggio in position, then groups up and down.
   Every chord right through the position, from its lowest note to its highest. The progression
   **Ted Greene: maj7 – A♭ D♭ G♭ B …** suits it: one kind of chord round in fourths.

### Group 2. Chord by chord

The chords of a tune, each on its own (**Over the changes**). You learn to find a chord where your
hand is. Move on when you get through the tune without a break at the chord changes.

1. From the root, 8 notes, ascending: two octaves of every chord.
2. 4 notes: one octave. Over quick changes, that is how arpeggios are really played.
3. **From the third**, then **In turn**: the chords in turn from `1`, `3`, `5` and `7`. You no
   longer go back to the root every time.
4. Direction **alternating**: up over one chord, down over the next.

### Group 3. One line

The line does not start again with every chord. You learn to enter the next chord from where the
last one ended. Move on when the line does not break at a change in two different positions.

1. Over the changes, **One line**, 4 notes. Every chord starts on its note nearest the one the
   chord before ended on, so the line flows through the whole tune.
2. **In one position**: the whole pass of the tune under one hand. After the pass the app moves you
   to another place on the neck. That is how Ted Greene taught playing through changes: the same
   tune in one position after another, until you see it without the paper.
3. **Ted Greene**, 8 or 4 notes: the arpeggio in position through the changes, then groups and
   permutations of a group. Last, **With tensions**. Start with a progression of two chords; the
   steps after that are in [How to practise, step by step](#how-to-practise-step-by-step).
4. Think of the next chord before you get there, as in
   [playing without the chord strip](intervals-practice.md#playing-without-the-chord-strip).

### Group 4. The language

Harris lines. First what lines are made of, then the lines: scales with a passing note, the
thirds and short phrases. You learn the material a solo is built from. There is no gate here: the
last step, a phrase of your own, is the aim.

1. **A key's chords**, the figures in turn: **Thirds**, **Triads**, **Seventh arpeggios**.
   The scale played in chords, degree by degree, in one position. Then another key.
2. **Thirds with a leading note**, **Arpeggios with a leading note** and **Arpeggios with a pivot**: the same cells with a
   half step before the first note, and with the root moved up an octave. They sound like a
   phrase already.
3. The same figures over **the tune's chords**, from the root and **from the third**: the arpeggio
   from a chord's third adds the ninth.
4. **Scale down** from the root, then as one line. The chord tones land on the strong beats.
5. **From the chord's third**, then **From the dominant's third** over a II–V–I. Listen to how they differ over the
   II and over the I.
6. **3rd into the root**, **Drop to the 3rd** and **Enclosure**, from the root and from the third.
7. The same figures **in one position** and as **one line**.
8. When a phrase sounds good, press pause and keep playing it your own way: from another note, in
   another rhythm, with another ending.

Each exercise is described in more detail below.

## Study in a key

A **study** is a written phrase, usually across two octaves, played over one chord. You pick the
study from the title above the exercise, and the key and the kind of chord in the quick options.

| study | notes | what it practises |
|---|---|---|
| Minor (Two Octaves and a Third) | 19 | two octaves and a third up, then down; over m7 |
| Major (Leading Tone) | 19 | up and down, ending on `7` just below the root; over maj7 |
| Dominant (Approach from Below) | 19 | up and down, ending by approaching the root from below; over 7 |
| Skipping Notes (Fifths and Fourths) | 30 | leaps of fifths and fourths |
| Triplets (Up-Down) | 47 | groups of three notes, up and down |
| Two Octaves Up-Down | 15 | two octaves up and back |
| Two Octaves Up | 8 | two octaves up |
| Two Octaves Down | 8 | two octaves down, from the seventh |
| Two Octaves Down from the Root | 8 | two octaves down, from the root |
| Groups of Four Up | 20 | groups of four notes, each from the next chord tone |
| Groups of Four Down | 20 | the same from the top down |
| Generator | – | a new phrase after every pass |

The first three studies set their chord too: Minor is played over m7, Major over maj7 and
Dominant over 7. The rest you can play over any chord.

**Random order** changes the key here: after every pass the study moves to a new one. The order of
the notes stays, because a study played in another order would not be that study any more.

## Over the changes

The chords come from a tune, and the app builds an arpeggio for each of them from the chord's own
notes. **Direction** decides whether it goes up, down, or alternately: up over one chord, down over
the next. Over C7:

| direction | C7 |
|---|---|
| ascending | `C E G B♭ C E G B♭`<br>`1 3 5 ♭7 1 3 5 ♭7` |
| descending | `C B♭ G E C B♭ G E`<br>`1 ♭7 5 3 1 ♭7 5 3` |

**Notes per chord**: 8 is two octaves, 4 is one. Two octaves teach the shape well, but through the
changes, especially where a chord lasts half a bar, one is what is usually played.

**Start** works as in the Harris lines: from the root, from the third, in turn (the chords in turn
from `1`, `3`, `5` and `7`) or one line, from the chord tone nearest the note the chord before ended
on. Over a II–V–I in C, 4 notes, up:

| start | Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| From the root | `D F  A C`<br>`1 ♭3 5 ♭7` | `G B D F`<br>`1 3 5 ♭7` | `C E G B`<br>`1 3 5 7` |
| From the third | `F  A C  D`<br>`♭3 5 ♭7 1` | `B D F  G`<br>`3 5 ♭7 1` | `E G B C`<br>`3 5 7 1` |
| In turn | `D F  A C`<br>`1 ♭3 5 ♭7` | `B D F  G`<br>`3 5 ♭7 1` | `G B C E`<br>`5 7 1 3` |
| One line | `F  A C  D`<br>`♭3 5 ♭7 1` | `B D F  G`<br>`3 5 ♭7 1` | `E G B C`<br>`3 5 7 1` |

From the root the line leaps at every change. As one line it moves into the next chord by a small
step: here through the thirds, `♭3`, `3`, `3`, as voice leading does.

Every chord is drawn close to where on the neck the one before ended. The first chord stands
somewhere else every time, and in one position the whole tune stands in one place, as described
below.

Over the changes, in the Harris lines and in Ted Greene's exercises **Chord context** draws the chord's notes around the line
in grey: under the hand in one position, otherwise where the line lies. The root and the rest of
the arpeggio stay in sight even where the figure does not play them. On by default.

## In one position

Over the changes and in the Harris lines there is **In one position**. Then the whole pass of
the tune is played under one hand: five frets, all the strings. The app fits every figure inside
them. Where the arpeggio from the chosen note does not fit, it starts on the nearest chord tone it
fits from. After every pass of the tune the app moves the position to another place on the neck.

**Where on the neck.** The app draws the position every time the exercise starts and after every
pass: from frets 0–4 to frets 8–12, that is the whole neck up to the twelfth fret. Ted Greene's
exercises always stand in a position. Over the changes and in the Harris lines **In one position**
decides, and it is on by default. With it off the line goes where its notes lead: through a tune
it keeps coming back to the nut, and the cells through a key's chords climb as far as the
seventeenth fret.

That is how Ted Greene taught playing through changes: the same tune in one position after another,
until you see each one without the paper. In a position what counts is not how many octaves an
arpeggio has, but what is under the hand: the chord's notes within a few frets.

## Ted Greene's exercises

Ted Greene practised arpeggios in position. He did not count how many octaves an arpeggio has; he
took every note of the chord that lies under the hand: five frets, all the strings. The exercise
stays in one position for the whole pass of the tune, and after every pass the app moves it to
another place on the neck. A short progression, of two chords or four, stays longer: the app moves
it only after eight chords, that is four times or twice round.

**Figures:**

| figure | what it does |
|---|---|
| **Arpeggio in position** | every note of the chord in the position, from the lowest to the highest and back |
| **Groups up** | the same cut into groups, each from the next chord tone; a group has 3, 4 or 5 notes |
| **Groups down** | the same from the top down |
| **Permutations of a group** | groups of four climb the position, each played in the same rearranged order; a new position brings the next order |

Over Cmaj7 in the 7th position:

| figure | Cmaj7, the whole position |
|---|---|
| Arpeggio in position | `B C E G B C E G B C B G E C B G E C B`<br>`7 1 3 5 7 1 3 5 7 1 7 5 3 1 7 5 3 1 7` |
| Groups up, of 4 | `B C E G · C E G B · E G B C · G B C E · B C E G · C E G B · E G B C`<br>`7 1 3 5 · 1 3 5 7 · 3 5 7 1 · 5 7 1 3 · 7 1 3 5 · 1 3 5 7 · 3 5 7 1` |
| Groups up, of 3 | `B C E · C E G · E G B · G B C · B C E · C E G · E G B · G B C`<br>`7 1 3 · 1 3 5 · 3 5 7 · 5 7 1 · 7 1 3 · 1 3 5 · 3 5 7 · 5 7 1` |
| Groups up, of 5 | `B C E G B · C E G B C · E G B C E · G B C E G · B C E G B · C E G B C`<br>`7 1 3 5 7 · 1 3 5 7 1 · 3 5 7 1 3 · 5 7 1 3 5 · 7 1 3 5 7 · 1 3 5 7 1` |

The neck shows the whole position, but only what you are playing now is in colour: one group, or
one way through the position; the rest of the figure is grey, and with **Chord context** on, the
rest of the position too, so with 8 or 4 notes a chord you still see the whole position. Above it the app says what it is,
for example "Group 3 of 7, down: 5 3 1 ♭7". A group played turns green and gives way to the next.
**Captions** in the quick options hides that line.

**Notes per chord:** 8, 4 or **The whole position**. With the whole position every chord plays the
whole figure: right through the position and back, or every group. That is how one chord is
practised. With 8 and 4 notes the line goes through the changes: every chord starts on its note
nearest the one the chord before ended on, and the line turns round at the edge of the position.

### Groups of 3, 4 and 5

On his sheet "Arpeggio Patterns" Greene wrote out groups of four through an arpeggio in position,
and added a note to try groups of 3, 2 and 5 notes as well, in 4/4, 12/8, 3/4 and 9/8. The list
**Groups of 3 / of 4 / of 5** appears with the figures **Groups up** and **Groups down**. There
are no groups of 2: each would begin on the note the last one ended on.

A chord takes whole groups only. With **8 notes** that is two groups of 3 or one of 5, with
**4 notes** one group of 3 or one of 5.

The app checks the order of the notes, not the rhythm. Choose the rhythm yourself: groups of 3 as
triplets or in 12/8, then as eighth notes in 4/4, where the accent falls on every third note and
walks through the bar. Groups of 5 in eighth notes shift the accent the other way.

### Permutations of a group

Four notes can be played in 24 orders. Greene wrote all of them out on his sheet "Permutations of
4-Note Groups" and asked for three things to be practised: the order, the place in the bar and
the speed. The app takes its numbering from him: order 1 runs from the top note of the group
straight down, order 24 from the bottom straight up, which is **Groups up**.

In the app every group of the position is played in the same order, so the hand learns one
pattern. Above the neck it says, for example, "Order 3 2 4 1 (9 of 24), group 1 of 2". The digits
count the notes of the group from the lowest: `3 2 4 1` is the third from the bottom, the second,
the highest and the lowest. When the app moves the exercise to a new position it takes the next
order, so after 24 positions you have played them all.

| order | Cmaj7, 7th position, the beginning |
|---|---|
| 1 of 24: `4 3 2 1` | `G E C B · C B G E · G E C B · C B G E`<br>`5 3 1 7 · 1 7 5 3 · 5 3 1 7 · 1 7 5 3` |
| 9 of 24: `3 2 4 1` | `E C G B · G E B C · B G C E · C B E G`<br>`3 1 5 7 · 5 3 7 1 · 7 5 1 3 · 1 7 3 5` |
| 16 of 24: `2 3 1 4` | `C E B G · E G C B · G B E C · B C G E`<br>`1 3 7 5 · 3 5 1 7 · 5 7 3 1 · 7 1 5 3` |

A group that would begin on the note just played, or on its octave, is passed over, and the next
one is taken. That is why in order 1 over a four-note chord the groups climb two chord tones at a
time: after `G E C B` the next would be `B G E C`, from the B an octave up.

Through the changes every chord takes its own group in the order of the position. Over
I–VI7–ii–V in C, 4 notes, order 9:

| | Cmaj7 | A7 | Dm7 | G7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Permutations of a group | `E C G B`<br>`3 1 5 7` | `G  E A C♯`<br>`♭7 5 1 3` | `F  D A C`<br>`♭3 1 5 ♭7` | `F  D G B`<br>`♭7 5 1 3` |

Then start the same order at another place in the bar: on the "and", on the second beat. That is
the second point on Greene's sheet, which the app does not check, and which turns the exercise
into a phrase.

### With tensions

**With tensions** adds the notes Greene added to his arpeggios: `9` and `6` over maj7, `9` and `13`
over 7, `9` and `11` over m7, `11` over m7♭5. In the app the ninth and thirteenth are written `2`
and `6`, or `9` and `13` with jazz notation on. Over Cmaj7 in the 7th position:

| | Cmaj7 with tensions |
|---|---|
| Arpeggio in position | `B C D E G A B C D E G A B C D C B A G E D C B A G E D C B`<br>`7 1 2 3 5 6 7 1 2 3 5 6 7 1 2 1 7 6 5 3 2 1 7 6 5 3 2 1 7` |

A richer arpeggio over a simpler chord sounds good: Greene suggested, for example, Em9 or Em11
over Em. The small shapes of such chords, five to seven notes each, he called condensed
arpeggios. The figure **Arpeggio in position** with tensions plays them all at once: each such
shape is a part of what the neck shows.

### Ted Greene's progressions

At the end of the list of tunes stand the progressions Greene set for practice. They come from
his sheets "Progressions for Soloing Over" and "Playing in One Position". They work in every mode
that reads tunes.

| title in the list | chords | what for |
|---|---|---|
| **I – IV7** | Cmaj7 F7 | two chords, one change: the first progression on the sheet |
| **I – ♭VII7** | Cmaj7 B♭7 | the same with the dominant a whole tone below |
| **I – ♭II7** | Cmaj7 D♭7 | and a half step above: hardly a note stays |
| **I – I7 – IV – ♭VII7** | Cmaj7 C7 Fmaj7 B♭7 | four chords, two keys |
| **I – vi – ii – V** | Cmaj7 Am7 Dm7 G7 | the turnaround in the key |
| **I – VI7 – ii – V** | Cmaj7 A7 Dm7 G7 | the same turnaround with a secondary dominant |
| **ii – V – I – VI7** | Dm7 G7 Cmaj7 A7 | the turnaround started on the ii |
| **ii – V – I – IV (C E A♭ F A)** | Cmaj7 Fmaj7, F♯m7 B7 Emaj7 Amaj7, B♭m7 E♭7 A♭maj7 D♭maj7, Gm7 C7 Fmaj7 B♭7, Bm7 E7 Amaj7 | one cadence through five keys |
| **maj7 – A♭ D♭ G♭ B …** | twelve maj7 chords round in fourths | one shape in every key, in one position |
| **7 – A♭ D♭ G♭ B …** | twelve dominants round in fourths | the same for 7 |
| **m7 – A♭ D♭ G♭ B …** | twelve m7 chords round in fourths | the same for m7 |

The chords are written as the app knows them. Greene wrote CΔ9 and F13 where the app has Cmaj7
and F7; **With tensions** brings the ninth and the thirteenth back. The altered dominants on his
sheets (G13♭9, A7♯9+) the app plays as plain 7, with the major ninth and the thirteenth. The
progressions with sus chords, and those made of altered dominants alone, are not in the list.

### How to practise, step by step

The order is taken from Greene's sheet "Suggested Practice": choose a position, find both chords
in it close to each other, play them, then make lines out of them. A few shapes in one position,
he wrote, will do the job as well as a hundred.

| step | settings | what should stay in the hand | when to move on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. One chord, the whole position | **maj7 – A♭ D♭ G♭ B …**, Arpeggio in position, The whole position | every note of the chord under the hand, up and down | when you play the chord before you look at the neck |
| 2. The same for 7 and m7 | the progressions **7 – …** and **m7 – …** | three kinds of chord in the same position | when you no longer mix up the third and the seventh |
| 3. Groups | Groups up, then down; of 4, then of 3 and of 5 | an arpeggio you can start from any of its notes | when the groups run evenly, with no gap between them |
| 4. Two chords | **I – IV7**, Arpeggio in position, 8 notes, then 4 | a change of chord without moving the hand | when you know which note you will enter the next chord on |
| 5. Harder pairs | **I – ♭VII7**, **I – ♭II7** | a change in which hardly anything stays | as above |
| 6. Four chords | **I – I7 – IV – ♭VII7**, then the three turnarounds | a line through the whole turnaround in one position | when the turnaround goes through in three different positions |
| 7. Groups and permutations through the changes | Groups, then Permutations of a group, 4 notes | a pattern you carry from chord to chord | when you keep the order without reading the caption |
| 8. With tensions | steps 1, 4 and 6 once more | the ninth and the thirteenth in the same shapes | when you hear which note is the tension |
| 9. Through the keys | **ii – V – I – IV (C E A♭ F A)**, then the standards | the same cadence wherever the hand happens to be | – |
| 10. A line of your own | pause | – | – |

Over two chords, 8 notes, with tensions, the line looks like this:

| | Cmaj7 | F7 | Cmaj7 | F7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arpeggio in position | `B C D E G A B C`<br>`7 1 2 3 5 6 7 1` | `D E♭ F G A C D C`<br>`6 ♭7 1 2 3 5 6 5` | `B A G E D C B A`<br>`7 6 5 3 2 1 7 6` | `G F E♭ D C D E♭ F`<br>`2 1 ♭7 6 5 6 ♭7 1` |

Every chord starts a step from the note the last one ended on: C goes to D, C to B, A to G. That
is what step 4 is about.

Step 10 Greene called what it is all about: out of the shapes you already see, make up musical
lines, weaving in other scale tones too. The app cannot judge that. Press pause, record the chords
or ask someone to accompany you, and play. Arpeggios, he wrote, are just a means to an end: after
a while you find yourself thinking in terms of melody alone.

Greene also asked for everything to be transposed to other keys at once. In Arpeggios the
progressions stand in C; the three progressions in fourths and the standards give the other keys.

## Harris lines

These exercises are inspired by the method of Barry Harris. Harris taught how to build flowing
eighth-note lines by adding passing notes to scales so that the chord tones land on the strong
beats. What matters is not the name of a scale but the rule that lets a line move through the
harmony in rhythm and with logic. You learn some of Harris's principles here, not his whole method
of improvising.

He also taught where exercises come from: phrases that already sound like music. With every figure
ask yourself: **would you play this in a solo?** The scale down and up teach where the notes are,
but they are not a phrase yet. Closer to music are the arpeggio with the scale, both thirds and the
enclosure. When a figure sounds good, press pause and keep playing it your own way: from another
note, in another rhythm, with another ending.

### First the cells: the scale played in chords

Harris would show a line, take it apart, and give an exercise for each part. An exercise should be
made of what lines are made of: the thirds, triads and seventh chords of a key, not scales run up
and down. The first six figures are those cells, in the order worth learning them in. Each grows
out of the one before: a third is two notes of a chord, a triad is two thirds, a seventh chord
three.

Over C major, degree by degree:

| figure | what it plays | how it begins in C major |
|---|---|---|
| **Thirds** | a note of the scale and the third above it | `C E`, `D F`, `E G`, `F A`… |
| **Triads** | the triad on every degree | `C E G`, `D F A`, `E G B`… |
| **Seventh arpeggios** | the seventh chord on every degree, note by note; Harris called them simply the chords | `C E G B`, `D F A C`, `E G B D`… |
| **Thirds with a leading note** | a third with the note a half step below leading into it | `B C E`, `C♯ D F`, `D♯ E G`… |
| **Arpeggios with a leading note** | the seventh chord with that leading note before its root: the bebop arpeggio | `B C E G B`, `C♯ D F A C`… |
| **Arpeggios with a pivot** | the root an octave up, the rest of the chord from below, and a step down the scale to end; Harris called it a pivot | `C' E G B A`, `D' F A C B`… |

The leading note is often outside the scale, which is why it pulls towards the next note: it is
what gives the bebop sound. The pivot arpeggio changes direction twice instead of once, so the
same four notes sound like a melody rather than an exercise.

**Rhythm.** The app checks the order of the notes, not the rhythm, but these figures have a rhythm
of their own and are worth playing in it. Triads are triplets. The bebop arpeggio is the leading
note on the "and", a triplet of the chord's first three notes, and the seventh on the next beat:
that is how Wes Montgomery's solo on Satin Doll begins. The pivot arpeggio sits well in three.

**A key's chords or the tune's.** The first list in the quick options of Harris lines chooses what
you play over.

- **A key's chords**: the seven seventh chords of a major scale, from the tonic to its octave, in
  the key from the list below it. The title reads "The chords of C major", and the chord strip
  shows the degrees: I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, viiø. Each chord gets one cell of the figure, so the
  figure goes through the scale as in the table. **Random key** moves the exercise to another key
  after every pass. With **In one position** the whole scale lies under one hand, and after a pass
  the app moves it elsewhere on the neck.
- **The tune's chords**: the same figures over the chords of a standard. A bar holds more than one
  cell. Thirds and triads go on up the scale, a step higher, as they do through a key, so the
  cells that follow share no note. The seventh arpeggio goes on up the chord: at **8 – a bar** the
  one from the root runs into the arpeggio from the third. The caption sets the cells apart with a
  dot, and the neck shows in colour only the one being played; the next waits in grey.

| figure, 8 notes | Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seventh arpeggios | `D F  A C  · F  A C  E`<br>`1 ♭3 5 ♭7 · ♭3 5 ♭7 2` | `G B D F  · B D F  A`<br>`1 3 5 ♭7 · 3 5 ♭7 2` | `C E G B · E G B D`<br>`1 3 5 7 · 3 5 7 2` |
| Triads | `D F  A · E G B`<br>`1 ♭3 5 · 2 4 6` | `G B D · A C E`<br>`1 3 5 · 2 4 6` | `C E G · D F A`<br>`1 3 5 · 2 4 6` |
| Thirds with a leading note | `C♯ D F  · D♯ E G`<br>`7  1 ♭3 · ♯1 2 4` | `F♯ G B · G♯ A C`<br>`7  1 3 · ♯1 2 4` | `B C E · C♯ D F`<br>`7 1 3 · ♯1 2 4` |

**The arpeggio from the third.** The start **From the third** builds the cell on the chord's
third. Over Dm7 the seventh chord from the third is F A C E, an Fmaj7; over G7 it is B D F A, over
Cmaj7 E G B D. Three notes are the chord's own and the fourth is the ninth, which is why the line
sounds fuller. Knowing a key's chords, you have these arpeggios under your fingers with nothing new
to learn. The bebop arpeggio from the third over Dm7 is `E F A C E`, the opening of that solo on
Satin Doll.

Over a chord that lies in the tune's key, the steps of the scale and the ninth come from that key:
over Em7 in C major the ninth is F, not F♯. Over a chord outside the key the figure takes that
chord's own plain scale.

The other figures in the list are lines. The rest of this chapter is about them.

### Where the extra half step comes from

A bar has eight eighth notes, and a plain scale has seven. Play C Mixolydian down from the root.
The strong beats are the first, third, fifth and seventh eighth notes:

| | C7 |
|---|---|
| a scale of 7 notes | `C B♭ A G F E D C`<br>`1 ♭7 6 5 4 3 2 1` |

On the strong beats are C, A, F and D, which is `1`, `6`, `4` and `2`. Only the root belongs to
the chord.

Add one chromatic note between `♭7` and the root. The scale now has eight notes:

| | C7 |
|---|---|
| a scale of 8 notes | `C B   B♭ A G F E D`<br>`1 (7) ♭7 6 5 4 3 2` |

Now the strong beats carry C, B♭, G and E: chord tones only. The note in brackets is the passing
note, which is not part of the scale. Chord tones and passing notes alternate, so from any chord
tone the line "comes out right".

### Every chord has its own scale of eight notes

| chord | scale | from C | on the strong beats |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | bebop dominant | `C D E F G A B♭ B`<br>`1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7 (7)` | C E G B♭ |
| maj7 | sixth diminished | `C D E F G G♯   A B`<br>`1 2 3 4 5 (♯5) 6 7` | C E G A |
| m6, m(maj7) | minor sixth diminished | `C D E♭ F G G♯   A B`<br>`1 2 ♭3 4 5 (♯5) 6 7` | C E♭ G A |
| m7 | the dominant a fourth up | `C D E♭ E   F G A B♭`<br>`1 2 ♭3 (3) 4 5 6 ♭7` | C E♭ F A |
| m7♭5 | the dominant a major third down | `C D♭ E♭ F G♭ G   A♭ B♭`<br>`1 ♭2 ♭3 4 ♭5 (5) ♭6 ♭7` | C E♭ G♭ A♭ |
| °7 | diminished | `C D E♭ F G♭ A♭ A   B`<br>`1 2 ♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭♭7 7` | C E♭ G♭ A |

Over maj7 the strong beats carry the sixth chord (`1 3 5 6`), with a diminished chord between
them. That is the scale Harris taught over major chords.

### Over m7 you think the dominant

The second big idea of Harris: **over m7 you play the dominant a fourth up**. Cm7 and F7 can use
the same line, because over Cm7 you think F7. The notes change their function against the chord,
but when the harmony changes you do not have to build a new phrase. The numbers show it: the same
note A is `6` over Cm7 and `3` over F7.

That is why the strong beats over Cm7 carry the notes of F7: C, A, F and E♭. Over Cm7 they are
`1`, `6`, `4` and `♭3`. The passing note is `(3)`, which is E: the major seventh of F7.

The same with m7♭5: Am7♭5 is the top of F9 (F A C E♭ G), so over Am7♭5 you think F7.

Look at the start of Autumn Leaves in the **From the dominant's third** figure. Over Cm7 and over F7 the
line is the same, because both times you think F7:

| figure | Cm7 | F7 | B♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| From the dominant's third | `A C E♭ G F E   E♭ D`<br>`6 1 ♭3 5 4 (3) ♭3 2` | `A C E♭ G F E   E♭ D`<br>`3 5 ♭7 2 1 (7) ♭7 6` | `D F G C B♭ A G F♯`<br>`3 5 6 2 1  7 6 (♯5)` |

### Figures

A **figure** is the path the line takes through the chord's scale. Each has eight notes, a bar of
eighth notes.

| figure | how it goes | example |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale down** | from a chord tone down the scale | `C B   B♭ A G F E D`<br>`1 (7) ♭7 6 5 4 3 2` |
| **Scale up** | from a chord tone up the scale | `F G A B♭ C D E♭ E`<br>`4 5 6 ♭7 1 2 ♭3 (3)` |
| **Arpeggio, scale down** | the arpeggio up, the octave on a strong beat, then down the scale | `F A C E♭ F E   E♭ D`<br>`1 3 5 ♭7 1 (7) ♭7 6` |
| **From the dominant's third** | the arpeggio from the third of the dominant you play over the chord: `3 5 7 9`, then down the scale | `A C E♭ G F E   E♭ D`<br>`6 1 ♭3 5 4 (3) ♭3 2` |
| **From the chord's third** | the arpeggio from the third of the chord itself: `3 5 7 9`, then down the scale | `F  A C  E D C  B A`<br>`♭3 5 ♭7 2 1 ♭7 6 5` |
| **Enclosure** | the note above the target, the note a half step below it, the target, the arpeggio to the seventh, a step down | `D B C E G B A`<br>`2 7 1 3 5 7 6` |
| **Drop to the 3rd** | the root, the half step under the third an octave down, the third, the seventh, two steps down | `C D♯ E B A G`<br>`1 ♯2 3 7 6 5` |
| **3rd into the root** | the third, the half step under the root, the note above it, the root | `E B D C`<br>`3 7 2 1` |

The examples are over C7, Cm7, F7, Cm7, Dm7 and three times over Cmaj7, in that order.

### Two thirds

The arpeggio from the third is a favourite move of Harris. It sounds richer than the arpeggio
from the root, because it ends on the ninth. The app has it in two versions.

**From the dominant's third** starts the arpeggio on the third of the dominant you play over the chord.
Over F7 that is A C E♭ G, the Am7♭5 chord. Over m7 the line thinks the dominant, so over Dm7 you play the arpeggio from the third
of G7: B D F A.

**From the chord's third** starts it on the third of the chord you play over. Over Dm7 that is F A C E, the Fmaj7
hidden in Dm7. Each chord then sounds like itself, and that is easier to hear.

Over a II–V–I in C:

| figure | Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| From the dominant's third | `B D F  A G F♯  F  E`<br>`6 1 ♭3 5 4 (3) ♭3 2` | `B D F  A G F♯  F  E`<br>`3 5 ♭7 2 1 (7) ♭7 6` | `E G A D C B A G♯`<br>`3 5 6 2 1 7 6 (♯5)` |
| From the chord's third | `F  A C  E D C  B A`<br>`♭3 5 ♭7 2 1 ♭7 6 5` | `B D F  A G F♯  F  E`<br>`3 5 ♭7 2 1 (7) ♭7 6` | `E G B D C B A G♯`<br>`3 5 7 2 1 7 6 (♯5)` |

Over G7 both play the same, because there the chord is the dominant. They differ over Dm7 and
over Cmaj7.

### Three phrases

The figures above are paths through the scale. The last three are different: short phrases of the
kind heard in bebop solos, turned into an exercise. Each starts on a chord tone, and each can be
played over every chord. They are built from the chord's own tones and its plain scale, without the
passing note.

**Enclosure.** Two approach notes come before the target: first the scale note above it, then the
note a half step below. Then the arpeggio up to the seventh and one step down the scale. The note
below the target is often outside the scale, and that is exactly why it pulls so hard towards it.
The app practises this one kind of enclosure; in improvising other shapes are used too.

**Drop to the 3rd.** The root, then a drop of nearly an octave to the note a half step below the
third, the third, a leap up to the seventh and two steps down the scale. That drop in the middle is
what makes it sound like a phrase rather than a scale.

**3rd into the root.** The third, then the root enclosed from both sides: the note a half step below
it, the scale note above it, and the root itself. Four notes, and already they sound like the end of
a phrase.

Over a II–V–I in C, from the root:

| phrase | Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enclosure | `E C♯ D F  A C  B`<br>`2 7  1 ♭3 5 ♭7 6` | `A F♯ G B D F  E`<br>`2 7  1 3 5 ♭7 6` | `D B C E G B A`<br>`2 7 1 3 5 7 6` |
| Drop to the 3rd | `D E F  C  B A`<br>`1 2 ♭3 ♭7 6 5` | `G A♯ B F  E D`<br>`1 ♯2 3 ♭7 6 5` | `C D♯ E B A G`<br>`1 ♯2 3 7 6 5` |
| 3rd into the root | `F  C♯ E D`<br>`♭3 7  2 1` | `B F♯ A G`<br>`3 7  2 1` | `E B D C`<br>`3 7 2 1` |

The **From the third** start builds the same phrase on the chord from its third: over Dm7 on Fmaj7,
over G7 on Bm7♭5, over Cmaj7 on Em7. The chord tones stay, and the ninth comes in:

| phrase from the third | Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enclosure | `G E F  A C  E D`<br>`4 2 ♭3 5 ♭7 2 1` | `C A♯ B D F  A G`<br>`4 ♯2 3 5 ♭7 2 1` | `F D♯ E G B D C`<br>`4 ♯2 3 5 7 2 1` |
| Drop to the 3rd | `F  G♯ A E D C`<br>`♭3 ♯4 5 2 1 ♭7` | `B C♯ D A G F`<br>`3 ♯4 5 2 1 ♭7` | `E F♯ G D C B`<br>`3 ♯4 5 2 1 7` |
| 3rd into the root | `A E G F`<br>`5 2 4 ♭3` | `D A♯ C B`<br>`5 ♯2 4 3` | `G D♯ F E`<br>`5 ♯2 4 3` |

The app credits a note outside the scale like any other.

### Start

**Start** says which chord tone the figure begins on.

| start | what it does |
|---|---|
| **From the root** | every chord from its root |
| **From the third** | every chord from its third |
| **In turn** | each chord from the next tone on a strong beat: root, third, then the other two |
| **One line** | one line through the whole tune, described below |

**From the dominant's third** and **From the chord's third** always begin on a third, whatever
the Start list says. For them the start chooses only
the octave. With the three phrases the start says which chord tone the phrase is built from.

This is **In turn** with the scale down, at the start of Autumn Leaves:

| | Cm7 | F7 | B♭maj7 | E♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| In turn | `C B♭ A G F E   E♭ D`<br>`1 ♭7 6 5 4 (3) ♭3 2` | `A G F E   E♭ D C B♭`<br>`3 2 1 (7) ♭7 6 5 4` | `F E♭ D C B♭ A G F♯`<br>`5 4  3 2 1  7 6 (♯5)` | `C B    B♭ A♭ G F E♭ D`<br>`6 (♯5) 5  4  3 2 1  7` |

### One line

The melodic line does not break off and start again at every chord; it moves smoothly into the
next one.
After the change it keeps going the same way and starts on the note of the new chord that lies
nearest the last one played and falls on a strong beat. When a scale reaches the end of the guitar's range, it turns round: the scale down becomes
the scale up and the other way about. That change of direction is forced by the range, not part of
the figure. The arpeggio with the scale climbs a little with every chord, so when it reaches the top
it begins again from the bottom.

The app picks the notes by simple rules of continuity. The line is always continuous, but not
every one will be equally musical: it is an exercise, not a finished phrase.

The drawing on the neck follows the line. Every chord is drawn where the line really is, so the hand
travels along the neck instead of standing at the nut.

| | Cm7 | F7 | B♭maj7 | E♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scale down | `E♭ D C B♭ A G F E`<br>`♭3 2 1 ♭7 6 5 4 (3)` | `E♭ D C B♭ A G F E`<br>`♭7 6 5 4  3 2 1 (7)` | `F F♯   G A B♭ C D E♭`<br>`5 (♯5) 6 7 1  2 3 4` | `C B    B♭ A♭ G F E♭ D`<br>`6 (♯5) 5  4  3 2 1  7` |
| Scale up | `F G A B♭ C D E♭ E`<br>`4 5 6 ♭7 1 2 ♭3 (3)` | `F G A B♭ C D E♭ E`<br>`1 2 3 4  5 6 ♭7 (7)` | `F F♯   G A B♭ C D E♭`<br>`5 (♯5) 6 7 1  2 3 4` | `C B    B♭ A♭ G F E♭ D`<br>`6 (♯5) 5  4  3 2 1  7` |

Look at the scale down. Cm7 ends on E (`(3)`), and F7 starts on E♭ (`♭7`), a half step lower. The
line does not jump, it carries on. At B♭maj7 the line is at the bottom of the range and turns
round: F7 ends on E, and B♭maj7 goes up from F (`5`). That is how a real bebop line through the
changes sounds.

Over Cm7 and F7 the line is the same, because over Cm7 you think F7. That is "over a II–V, play
the V" in practice.

### Eight notes or four

In standards a chord usually lasts a bar or half a bar. **8 – a bar** plays the whole figure, and
**4 – half a bar** its first half. The first six figures are played in whole cells: as many as
eight or four notes hold, and always one at least. Over a key's chords every chord gets one cell,
and the list is not there. Where a chord lasts two bars, the tune writes it twice, like
Gm7 Gm7 at the end of Autumn Leaves.

### Tempo

The app sets no tempo. It waits for you to play the next note, so you play at your own pace, as
in the other modes.

The speed of playing is limited, though, by how fast the app recognises notes. In the tests so
far a safe starting point was about half a second a note. That is a limit of the app, not the tempo
you need to master the line at. Play faster and the line stops on a note the app had no time to
credit. Tested on lines spliced from recorded notes: at half a second
a note the falling lines went through every time, half steps included, while the rising ones
sometimes stopped on a note. At 0.4 s the lines stop. The details are in
[the judge's measurements](judge-measurements.md), under *Lines in order*.

If you want to play a line to a metronome, eighth notes at about 60 beats a minute fit in that
time. That is a hint, not a requirement.
