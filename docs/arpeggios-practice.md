# Practising arpeggios

Arpeggios contains three exercises: a study in a key, over the changes, and Harris lines. The
description below explains examples printed by the app itself.

[← back to the README](../README.md)

---

## A few words first

An **arpeggio** is the notes of a chord played one after another instead of together. The C7
chord is C E G B♭, and its arpeggio is the same notes played as a line.

As in Intervals, the notes are written as **numbers counted from the root of the chord**. `3` is
the third of the chord on screen: in C7 that is E, in Cm7 E♭. In the tables each note name has its
number under it, so the numbers soon feel familiar.

You choose the exercise in the quick options of Arpeggios or in Settings, on the Practice tab.

## Study in a key

A **study** is a written phrase, usually across two octaves, played over one chord. You pick the
study from the title above the exercise, and the key and the kind of chord in the quick options.

| study | notes | what it practises |
|---|---|---|
| Minor (Two Octaves and a Third) | 19 | two octaves and a third up, then down; over m7 |
| Major (Leading Tone) | 19 | up and down, ending on `7` just below the root; over maj7 |
| Dominant (Approach from Below) | 19 | up and down, ending by approaching the root from below; over 7 |
| Skipping Notes (Fifths and Fourths) | 30 | leaps of fifths and fourths |
| Triplets (Up-Down) | 47 | groups of three notes, up and down |
| Two Octaves Up-Down | 15 | two octaves up and back |
| Two Octaves Up | 8 | two octaves up |
| Two Octaves Down | 8 | two octaves down, from the seventh |
| Two Octaves Down from the Root | 8 | two octaves down, from the root |
| Generator | – | a new phrase after every pass |

The first three studies set their chord too: Minor is played over m7, Major over maj7 and
Dominant over 7. The rest you can play over any chord.

**Random order** changes the key here: after every pass the study moves to a new one. The order of
the notes stays, because a study played in another order would not be that study any more.

## Over the changes

The chords come from a tune, and the app builds an arpeggio for each of them: the chord's notes
across two octaves. **Direction** decides whether it goes up, down, or alternately: up over one
chord, down over the next. Over C7:

| direction | C7 |
|---|---|
| ascending | `C E G B♭ C E G B♭`<br>`1 3 5 ♭7 1 3 5 ♭7` |
| descending | `C B♭ G E C B♭ G E`<br>`1 ♭7 5 3 1 ♭7 5 3` |

Every chord is drawn close to where on the neck the one before ended. The first chord stands
somewhere else every time.

It is a good exercise for finding every chord on the neck. It has one drawback, though: every chord
starts on its root, so the line jumps at every change. The next exercise is about that.

## Harris lines

These exercises are inspired by the method of Barry Harris. Harris taught how to build flowing
eighth-note lines by adding passing notes to scales so that the chord tones land on the strong
beats. What matters is not the name of a scale but the rule that lets a line move through the
harmony in rhythm and with logic. You learn some of Harris's principles here, not his whole method
of improvising.

He also taught where exercises come from: phrases that already sound like music. With every figure
ask yourself: **would you play this in a solo?** The scale down and up teach where the notes are,
but they are not a phrase yet. Closer to music are the arpeggio with the scale, both thirds and the
enclosure. When a figure sounds good, press pause and keep playing it your own way: from another
note, in another rhythm, with another ending.

### Where the extra half step comes from

A bar has eight eighth notes, and a plain scale has seven. Play C Mixolydian down from the root.
The strong beats are the first, third, fifth and seventh eighth notes:

| | C7 |
|---|---|
| a scale of 7 notes | `C B♭ A G F E D C`<br>`1 ♭7 6 5 4 3 2 1` |

On the strong beats are C, A, F and D, which is `1`, `6`, `4` and `2`. Only the root belongs to
the chord.

Add one chromatic note between `♭7` and the root. The scale now has eight notes:

| | C7 |
|---|---|
| a scale of 8 notes | `C B   B♭ A G F E D`<br>`1 (7) ♭7 6 5 4 3 2` |

Now the strong beats carry C, B♭, G and E: chord tones only. The note in brackets is the passing
note, which is not part of the scale. Chord tones and passing notes alternate, so from any chord
tone the line "comes out right".

### Every chord has its own scale of eight notes

| chord | scale | from C | on the strong beats |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | bebop dominant | `C D E F G A B♭ B`<br>`1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7 (7)` | C E G B♭ |
| maj7 | sixth diminished | `C D E F G G♯   A B`<br>`1 2 3 4 5 (♯5) 6 7` | C E G A |
| m6, m(maj7) | minor sixth diminished | `C D E♭ F G G♯   A B`<br>`1 2 ♭3 4 5 (♯5) 6 7` | C E♭ G A |
| m7 | the dominant a fourth up | `C D E♭ E   F G A B♭`<br>`1 2 ♭3 (3) 4 5 6 ♭7` | C E♭ F A |
| m7♭5 | the dominant a major third down | `C D♭ E♭ F G♭ G   A♭ B♭`<br>`1 ♭2 ♭3 4 ♭5 (5) ♭6 ♭7` | C E♭ G♭ A♭ |
| °7 | diminished | `C D E♭ F G♭ A♭ A   B`<br>`1 2 ♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭♭7 7` | C E♭ G♭ A |

Over maj7 the strong beats carry the sixth chord (`1 3 5 6`), with a diminished chord between
them. That is the scale Harris taught over major chords.

### Over m7 you think the dominant

The second big idea of Harris: **over m7 you play the dominant a fourth up**. Cm7 and F7 can use
the same line, because over Cm7 you think F7. The notes change their function against the chord,
but when the harmony changes you do not have to build a new phrase. The numbers show it: the same
note A is `6` over Cm7 and `3` over F7.

That is why the strong beats over Cm7 carry the notes of F7: C, A, F and E♭. Over Cm7 they are
`1`, `6`, `4` and `♭3`. The passing note is `(3)`, which is E: the major seventh of F7.

The same with m7♭5: Am7♭5 is the top of F9 (F A C E♭ G), so over Am7♭5 you think F7.

Look at the start of Autumn Leaves in the **Dominant's third** figure. Over Cm7 and over F7 the
line is the same, because both times you think F7:

| figure | Cm7 | F7 | B♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dominant's third | `A C E♭ G F E   E♭ D`<br>`6 1 ♭3 5 4 (3) ♭3 2` | `A C E♭ G F E   E♭ D`<br>`3 5 ♭7 2 1 (7) ♭7 6` | `D F G C B♭ A G F♯`<br>`3 5 6 2 1  7 6 (♯5)` |

### Figures

A **figure** is the path the line takes through the chord's scale. Each has eight notes, a bar of
eighth notes.

| figure | how it goes | example |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale down** | from a chord tone down the scale | `C B   B♭ A G F E D`<br>`1 (7) ♭7 6 5 4 3 2` |
| **Scale up** | from a chord tone up the scale | `F G A B♭ C D E♭ E`<br>`4 5 6 ♭7 1 2 ♭3 (3)` |
| **Arpeggio, scale down** | the arpeggio up, the octave on a strong beat, then down the scale | `F A C E♭ F E   E♭ D`<br>`1 3 5 ♭7 1 (7) ♭7 6` |
| **Dominant's third** | the arpeggio from the dominant's third: `3 5 7 9`, then down the scale | `A C E♭ G F E   E♭ D`<br>`6 1 ♭3 5 4 (3) ♭3 2` |
| **Chord's third** | the arpeggio from the third of the chord itself: `3 5 7 9`, then down the scale | `F  A C  E D C  B A`<br>`♭3 5 ♭7 2 1 ♭7 6 5` |
| **Enclosure** | the note above the target, the note a half step below it, the target, the arpeggio to the seventh, a step down | `D B C E G B A`<br>`2 7 1 3 5 7 6` |
| **Drop to the 3rd** | the root, the half step under the third an octave down, the third, the seventh, two steps down | `C D♯ E B A G`<br>`1 ♯2 3 7 6 5` |
| **3rd into the root** | the third, the half step under the root, the note above it, the root | `E B D C`<br>`3 7 2 1` |

The examples are over C7, Cm7, F7, Cm7, Dm7 and three times over Cmaj7, in that order.

### Two thirds

The arpeggio from the third is a favourite move of Harris. It sounds richer than the arpeggio
from the root, because it ends on the ninth. The app has it in two versions.

**Dominant's third** starts the arpeggio on the third of the dominant you play over the chord.
Over F7 that is A C E♭ G, the Am7♭5 chord. Over m7 the line thinks the dominant, so over Dm7 you play the arpeggio from the third
of G7: B D F A.

**Chord's third** takes the third of the chord you play over. Over Dm7 that is F A C E, the Fmaj7
hidden in Dm7. Each chord then sounds like itself, and that is easier to hear.

Over a II–V–I in C:

| figure | Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dominant's third | `B D F  A G F♯  F  E`<br>`6 1 ♭3 5 4 (3) ♭3 2` | `B D F  A G F♯  F  E`<br>`3 5 ♭7 2 1 (7) ♭7 6` | `E G A D C B A G♯`<br>`3 5 6 2 1 7 6 (♯5)` |
| Chord's third | `F  A C  E D C  B A`<br>`♭3 5 ♭7 2 1 ♭7 6 5` | `B D F  A G F♯  F  E`<br>`3 5 ♭7 2 1 (7) ♭7 6` | `E G B D C B A G♯`<br>`3 5 7 2 1 7 6 (♯5)` |

Over G7 both play the same, because there the chord is the dominant. They differ over Dm7 and
over Cmaj7.

### Three phrases

The figures above are paths through the scale. The last three are different: short phrases of the
kind heard in bebop solos, turned into an exercise. Each starts on a chord tone, and each can be
played over every chord. They are built from the chord's own tones and its plain scale, without the
passing note.

**Enclosure.** Two approach notes come before the target: first the scale note above it, then the
note a half step below. Then the arpeggio up to the seventh and one step down the scale. The note
below the target is often outside the scale, and that is exactly why it pulls so hard towards it.
The app practises this one kind of enclosure; in improvising other shapes are used too.

**Drop to the 3rd.** The root, then a drop of nearly an octave to the note a half step below the
third, the third, a leap up to the seventh and two steps down the scale. That drop in the middle is
what makes it sound like a phrase rather than a scale.

**3rd into the root.** The third, then the root enclosed from both sides: the note a half step below
it, the scale note above it, and the root itself. Four notes, and already they sound like the end of
a phrase.

Over a II–V–I in C, from the root:

| phrase | Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enclosure | `E C♯ D F  A C  B`<br>`2 7  1 ♭3 5 ♭7 6` | `A F♯ G B D F  E`<br>`2 7  1 3 5 ♭7 6` | `D B C E G B A`<br>`2 7 1 3 5 7 6` |
| Drop to the 3rd | `D E F  C  B A`<br>`1 2 ♭3 ♭7 6 5` | `G A♯ B F  E D`<br>`1 ♯2 3 ♭7 6 5` | `C D♯ E B A G`<br>`1 ♯2 3 7 6 5` |
| 3rd into the root | `F  C♯ E D`<br>`♭3 7  2 1` | `B F♯ A G`<br>`3 7  2 1` | `E B D C`<br>`3 7 2 1` |

The **From the third** start builds the same phrase on the chord from its third: over Dm7 on Fmaj7,
over G7 on Bm7♭5, over Cmaj7 on Em7. The chord tones stay, and the ninth comes in:

| phrase from the third | Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enclosure | `G E F  A C  E D`<br>`4 2 ♭3 5 ♭7 2 1` | `C A♯ B D F  A G`<br>`4 ♯2 3 5 ♭7 2 1` | `F D♯ E G B D C`<br>`4 ♯2 3 5 7 2 1` |
| Drop to the 3rd | `F  G♯ A E D C`<br>`♭3 ♯4 5 2 1 ♭7` | `B C♯ D A G F`<br>`3 ♯4 5 2 1 ♭7` | `E F♯ G D C B`<br>`3 ♯4 5 2 1 7` |
| 3rd into the root | `A E G F`<br>`5 2 4 ♭3` | `D A♯ C B`<br>`5 ♯2 4 3` | `G D♯ F E`<br>`5 ♯2 4 3` |

The app credits a note outside the scale like any other.

### Start

**Start** says which chord tone the figure begins on.

| start | what it does |
|---|---|
| **From the root** | every chord from its root |
| **From the third** | every chord from its third |
| **In turn** | each chord from the next tone on a strong beat: root, third, then the other two |
| **One line** | one line through the whole tune, described below |

**Dominant's third** and **Chord's third** always begin on a third. For them the start chooses only
the octave. With the three phrases the start says which chord tone the phrase is built from.

This is **In turn** with the scale down, at the start of Autumn Leaves:

| | Cm7 | F7 | B♭maj7 | E♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| In turn | `C B♭ A G F E   E♭ D`<br>`1 ♭7 6 5 4 (3) ♭3 2` | `A G F E   E♭ D C B♭`<br>`3 2 1 (7) ♭7 6 5 4` | `F E♭ D C B♭ A G F♯`<br>`5 4  3 2 1  7 6 (♯5)` | `C B    B♭ A♭ G F E♭ D`<br>`6 (♯5) 5  4  3 2 1  7` |

### One line

This is the start that matters most. The line does not begin again at every chord. After the
change it plays the new chord's tone on a strong beat nearest the note it ended on, carrying on the
same way. When a scale reaches the end of the guitar's range, it turns round: the scale down becomes
the scale up and the other way about. That change of direction is forced by the range, not part of
the figure. The arpeggio with the scale climbs a little with every chord, so when it reaches the top
it begins again from the bottom.

The app picks the notes by simple rules of continuity. The line is always continuous, but not
every one will be equally musical: it is an exercise, not a finished phrase.

The drawing on the neck follows the line. Every chord is drawn where the line really is, so the hand
travels along the neck instead of standing at the nut.

| | Cm7 | F7 | B♭maj7 | E♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scale down | `E♭ D C B♭ A G F E`<br>`♭3 2 1 ♭7 6 5 4 (3)` | `E♭ D C B♭ A G F E`<br>`♭7 6 5 4  3 2 1 (7)` | `F F♯   G A B♭ C D E♭`<br>`5 (♯5) 6 7 1  2 3 4` | `C B    B♭ A♭ G F E♭ D`<br>`6 (♯5) 5  4  3 2 1  7` |
| Scale up | `F G A B♭ C D E♭ E`<br>`4 5 6 ♭7 1 2 ♭3 (3)` | `F G A B♭ C D E♭ E`<br>`1 2 3 4  5 6 ♭7 (7)` | `F F♯   G A B♭ C D E♭`<br>`5 (♯5) 6 7 1  2 3 4` | `C B    B♭ A♭ G F E♭ D`<br>`6 (♯5) 5  4  3 2 1  7` |

Look at the scale down. Cm7 ends on E (`(3)`), and F7 starts on E♭ (`♭7`), a half step lower. The
line does not jump, it carries on. At B♭maj7 the line is at the bottom of the range and turns
round: F7 ends on E, and B♭maj7 goes up from F (`5`). That is how a real bebop line through the
changes sounds.

Over Cm7 and F7 the line is the same, because over Cm7 you think F7. That is "over a II–V, play
the V" in practice.

### Eight notes or four

In standards a chord usually lasts a bar or half a bar. **8 – a bar** plays the whole figure, and
**4 – half a bar** its first half. Where a chord lasts two bars, the tune writes it twice, like
Gm7 Gm7 at the end of Autumn Leaves.

### Tempo

The app sets no tempo. It waits for you to play the next note, so you play at your own pace, as
in the other modes.

The speed of playing is limited, though, by how fast the app recognises notes. In the tests so
far a safe starting point was about half a second a note. That is a limit of the app, not the tempo
you need to master the line at. Play faster and the line stops on a note the app had no time to
credit. Tested on lines spliced from recorded notes: at half a second
a note the falling lines went through every time, half steps included, while the rising ones
sometimes stopped on a note. At 0.4 s the lines stop. The details are in
[the judge's measurements](judge-measurements.md), under *Lines in order*.

If you want to play a line to a metronome, eighth notes at about 60 beats a minute fit in that
time. That is a hint, not a requirement.

## Where to start

1. **Over the changes**, ascending, in a tune you know. Find every chord on the neck.
2. **Harris lines**, **Scale down**, **From the root**. Listen to the chord tones landing on the
   strong beats, and visualise them on the neck.
3. The same **From the third**, then **In turn**.
4. **Chord's third** over a II–V–I, then **Dominant's third**. Listen to how they differ over the
   II and over the I.
5. **3rd into the root**, **Drop to the 3rd** and **Enclosure**, from the root, then from the
   third. Listen to the note a half step below pulling into the target. When a phrase sounds
   good, play it on the pause your own way.
6. **One line** with the scale down, then up. Think of the next chord before you get there, as in
   [playing without the chord strip](intervals-practice.md#playing-without-the-chord-strip).
7. **Arpeggio, scale down** or one of the phrases with one line, and last with four notes a chord.
