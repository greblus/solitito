# Practising intervals

What each of the fifty exercises in Intervals is about. Every example below was printed by the
app itself, so it is exactly what the app expects you to play.

[← back to the README](../README.md)

---

## A few words first

A **chord** is a few notes that fit together and sound together. Every chord has a main note it
takes its name from. In a C chord that note is C. It is called the **root**.

A **degree** is the running number of a note in the scale, and so in the chord too, counted from
the root. The root is `1`. Then you count on: `2`,
`3`, `4`, `5` and so on. A four-note chord is usually made of degrees `1 3 5 7`.

The most important thing: **an exercise is written in numbers, not in note names.** `3` means
"the third of the chord on screen right now". In C maj7 that is E, in Cm7 it is E♭. So one
exercise fits every chord in a song. The grip on the neck changes, but the sound of "the three"
stays. That is what this is about: visualising the neck and hearing intervals.

A small sign next to a number moves it by one fret: `♭3` is one fret lower than `3`, `♯4` one fret
higher than `4`.

## Where the notes come from

`1`, `3`, `5` and `7` are always notes of the chord itself.

`2`, `4` and `6` are notes from outside the chord. They come from its **scale**, a row of
notes that sound good with that chord. Every kind of chord has its own. Here and in the tables
further on, each note name has its number under it, counted from the root of the chord, so the
numbers soon feel familiar:

| chord | scale | notes from C |
|---|---|---|
| maj7 | Lydian | `C D E F♯ G A B`<br>`1 2 3 ♯4 5 6 7` |
| 7 | Mixolydian | `C D E F G A B♭`<br>`1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7` |
| m7 | Dorian | `C D E♭ F G A B♭`<br>`1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7` |
| m7♭5 | Locrian ♮9 | `C D E♭ F G♭ A♭ B♭`<br>`1 2 ♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭7` |
| °7 | whole-half diminished | `C D E♭ F G♭ A♭ A`<br>`1 2 ♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭♭7` |
| m6, m(maj7) | melodic minor | `C D E♭ F G A B`<br>`1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 7` |

In the suspension exercises `4` over maj7 is the plain F, not F♯.

You don't need to learn these names. The app picks the scale for the chord by itself.

## Playing without the chord strip

At first, the exercises with the intervals shown on the neck are there to help you understand
them, build your imagination and learn to see them on the neck. The strip at the top of the window
shows the chord you are on and the ones coming next, so you get used to the song's chords. The aim,
though, is to work without the chord strip: by feel, from memory. It is a long road, and the
author is only at the start of it too.

Reading a chord off the strip and working out where the intervals you want to play lie takes a
long time, and it makes you stall while playing. The next chord lands on the player as a surprise,
and that surprise is what we want to avoid.

So the goal is to play with your head one step ahead. While you play over one chord, you are
already thinking of the next one, and you know which notes you will play there before you get
there.

Take **Two tones: 3 5** in All the Things You Are. It starts like this:

| chord | Fm7 | B♭m7 | E♭7 | A♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `3 5` | `A♭ C`<br>`♭3 5` | `D♭ F`<br>`♭3 5` | `G B♭`<br>`3 5` | `C E♭`<br>`3 5` |

While your fingers play A♭ and C over Fm7 (`♭3` and `5`), your head is already on B♭m7: D♭ and F
(`♭3` and `5` again). When you get there, it moves on to E♭7: G and B♭ (`3` and `5`). Nothing catches
you by surprise.

One day you will be able to plan the notes of the next two or five chords and improvise freely
between them. The next chord to play will stop taking up your attention, and you will have
time left to choose where on the neck you play them.

How to get there:

1. Play with the strip on until the song's chords feel familiar.
2. Turn **Chord strip** off in the quick options of the mode or in Settings, on the Practice tab.
   Only the current chord is left on screen.
3. Before each chord changes, name the next one to yourself, and its notes. Go slowly: if you get
   lost, turn the strip back on for a while.
4. When the next chord comes easily, try to think two chords ahead.

## Reading the list of exercises

Each entry on the list has a group name and the note numbers. The numbers at the start are for
major and dominant chords, like C maj7 or C7. Sometimes `m:` or `°:` follows:

```
Structure 1–5: 1 2 3 5 · m: 1 b3 4 5
```

- after **`m:`** come the numbers for minor chords (like Cm7), written as a minor chord has them:
  `b3`, `b7`. Half-diminished chords (like Cm7♭5) use them too; there the `5` is a `b5`;
- after **`°:`** come the numbers for diminished chords (like C°7), with `b3`, `b5` and `bb7`.

Why? In a minor chord `2` sits right next to `b3`, just one fret away. Two notes that close sound
sharp and harsh together. So in a minor chord you play `4` instead of `2`. Over Cm7 that entry is:

```
C E♭ F G
1 ♭3 4 5
```

The arrow **↻** means a **random inversion**: the chord started from a note other than the root.
The app draws the inversion again for every chord. For example, `1 3 5 ↻` may come as `3 5 1`
over one chord and as `5 1 3` over the next.

**Voice leading** is numbered 1, 2, 3… instead of spelled out. Its notes change with every chord,
so a single line of numbers would explain nothing.

## The exercises, one group at a time

The examples are for chords on C: C maj7, C7, Cm7, Cm7♭5 and C°7.

### One, two and three tones

The simplest ones: one note of the chord, two or three. You play them one at a time.

They are **sets**. With the **In order** switch off you may play the notes of a set in any order.
With it on, you play them in the order on screen.

| exercise | C maj7 | C7 | Cm7 | Cm7♭5 | C°7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `3` | `E`<br>`3` | `E`<br>`3` | `E♭`<br>`♭3` | `E♭`<br>`♭3` | `E♭`<br>`♭3` |
| `3 7` | `E B`<br>`3 7` | `E B♭`<br>`3 ♭7` | `E♭ B♭`<br>`♭3 ♭7` | `E♭ B♭`<br>`♭3 ♭7` | `E♭ A`<br>`♭3 ♭♭7` |
| `1 3 5` | `C E G`<br>`1 3 5` | `C E G`<br>`1 3 5` | `C E♭ G`<br>`1 ♭3 5` | `C E♭ G♭`<br>`1 ♭3 ♭5` | `C E♭ G♭`<br>`1 ♭3 ♭5` |
| `3 5 7` | `E G B`<br>`3 5 7` | `E G B♭`<br>`3 5 ♭7` | `E♭ G B♭`<br>`♭3 5 ♭7` | `E♭ G♭ B♭`<br>`♭3 ♭5 ♭7` | `E♭ G♭ A`<br>`♭3 ♭5 ♭♭7` |

`3 7` are the two most important notes of a chord. They tell you what chord it is: major or minor,
and with which seventh. Start with them.

### Four tones

The whole four-note chord, then the same chord started from another note:

| exercise | C maj7 | Cm7 | C°7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| `1 3 5 7` | `C E G B`<br>`1 3 5 7` | `C E♭ G B♭`<br>`1 ♭3 5 ♭7` | `C E♭ G♭ A`<br>`1 ♭3 ♭5 ♭♭7` |
| `3 5 7 1` | `E G B C`<br>`3 5 7 1` | `E♭ G B♭ C`<br>`♭3 5 ♭7 1` | `E♭ G♭ A   C`<br>`♭3 ♭5 ♭♭7 1` |
| `5 7 1 3` | `G B C E`<br>`5 7 1 3` | `G B♭ C E♭`<br>`5 ♭7 1 ♭3` | `G♭ A   C E♭`<br>`♭5 ♭♭7 1 ♭3` |
| `7 1 3 5` | `B C E G`<br>`7 1 3 5` | `B♭ C E♭ G`<br>`♭7 1 ♭3 5` | `A   C E♭ G♭`<br>`♭♭7 1 ♭3 ♭5` |

The last four exercises of the group have five notes: they end with the one they started on once
more, for example `1 3 5 7 1`. Only `1 3 5 7` is a set. The rest you always play in the order on
screen.

### Structure 1–5 and Structure 5–9

Four notes that together sound like a little tune. Each structure comes in four orders and as a
random inversion (↻). From the root first:

| exercise | C maj7, C7 | Cm7 | Cm7♭5 | C°7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `1 2 3 5` | `C D E G`<br>`1 2 3 5` | `C E♭ F G`<br>`1 ♭3 4 5` | `C E♭ F G♭`<br>`1 ♭3 4 ♭5` | `C D E♭ G♭`<br>`1 2 ♭3 ♭5` |
| `2 1 5 3` | `D C G E`<br>`2 1 5 3` | `E♭ C G F`<br>`♭3 1 5 4` | `E♭ C G♭ F`<br>`♭3 1 ♭5 4` | `D C G♭ E♭`<br>`2 1 ♭5 ♭3` |
| `3 5 2 1` | `E G D C`<br>`3 5 2 1` | `F G E♭ C`<br>`4 5 ♭3 1` | `F G♭ E♭ C`<br>`4 ♭5 ♭3 1` | `E♭ G♭ D C`<br>`♭3 ♭5 2 1` |
| `5 1 2 3` | `G C D E`<br>`5 1 2 3` | `G C E♭ F`<br>`5 1 ♭3 4` | `G♭ C E♭ F`<br>`♭5 1 ♭3 4` | `G♭ C D E♭`<br>`♭5 1 2 ♭3` |

Structure 5–9 is the same shape started from the fifth note. Your hand plays what it already
knows, but you hear quite different, richer notes:

| exercise | C maj7 | C7 | Cm7 | C°7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `5 6 7 2` | `G A B D`<br>`5 6 7 2` | `G A B♭ D`<br>`5 6 ♭7 2` | `G B♭ C D`<br>`5 ♭7 1 2` | `G♭ A♭ A   D`<br>`♭5 ♭6 ♭♭7 2` |
| `6 5 2 7` | `A G D B`<br>`6 5 2 7` | `A G D B♭`<br>`6 5 2 ♭7` | `B♭ G D C`<br>`♭7 5 2 1` | `A♭ G♭ D A`<br>`♭6 ♭5 2 ♭♭7` |

### Voice leading

Here you practise moving from one chord to the next. The last note over one chord lies right next
to the first note over the next one, two frets away at most. So the melody flows from chord to
chord instead of jumping.

The example is the start of Autumn Leaves, the chords Cm7, F7 and B♭maj7:

| exercise | Cm7 | F7 | B♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voice leading 1 | `E♭ D C B♭`<br>`♭3 2 1 ♭7` | `A G F E♭`<br>`3 2 1 ♭7` | `D C B♭ A`<br>`3 2 1  7` |
| Voice leading 2 | `E♭ D C B♭`<br>`♭3 2 1 ♭7` | `A C E♭ G`<br>`3 5 ♭7 2` | `F B♭ C D`<br>`5 1  2 3` |
| Voice leading 3 | `C E♭ G B♭`<br>`1 ♭3 5 ♭7` | `A G F E♭`<br>`3 2 1 ♭7` | `D C B♭ A`<br>`3 2 1  7` |
| Voice leading 4 | `C E♭ G B♭`<br>`1 ♭3 5 ♭7` | `A C E♭ G`<br>`3 5 ♭7 2` | `D C B♭ A`<br>`3 2 1  7` |
| Voice leading 5 | `G E♭ C B♭`<br>`5 ♭3 1 ♭7` | `A C E♭ G`<br>`3 5 ♭7 2` | `D C B♭ A`<br>`3 2 1  7` |

Look at the first row. Over Cm7 you end on B♭ (`♭7`), and over F7 you start on A (`3`), one fret
lower. Over F7 you end on E♭ (`♭7`), and over B♭maj7 you start on D (`3`), one fret lower again.
Played fast, it sounds like a real jazz line.

These exercises only work with the chords in the song's order. That is why the shuffle does not
change the order of their notes.

### Suspension resolved

You start on a note that sounds a little "up in the air", as if waiting for something. Then you
step down to `3`, which sounds settled, and end on the root:

| exercise | Cm7 | F7 | B♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| `2 3 1` | `D E♭ C`<br>`2 ♭3 1` | `G A F`<br>`2 3 1` | `C D B♭`<br>`2 3 1` |
| `4 3 1` | `F E♭ C`<br>`4 ♭3 1` | `B♭ A F`<br>`4  3 1` | `E♭ D B♭`<br>`4  3 1` |

### Chord scale and passing-note scale

The **chord scale** is every note of the chord's scale, in a row, across a whole octave. You can
start on `1`, `3`, `5`, `7` or on a note the app draws for you. For example from `3`:

| chord | Chord scale: 3 |
|---|---|
| C maj7 | `E F♯ G A B C D E`<br>`3 ♯4 5 6 7 1 2 3` |
| C7 | `E F G A B♭ C D E`<br>`3 4 5 6 ♭7 1 2 3` |
| Cm7 | `E♭ F G A B♭ C D E♭`<br>`♭3 4 5 6 ♭7 1 2 ♭3` |
| Cm7♭5 | `E♭ F G♭ A♭ B♭ C D E♭`<br>`♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭7 1 2 ♭3` |

The **passing-note scale** has one extra note, in brackets. With it the scale has eight
notes instead of seven. Play it evenly, two notes to each beat, and the chord's notes always land
on the beat:

| chord | Passing-note scale: 1 |
|---|---|
| C maj7 | `C D E F♯ G (G♯) A B C`<br>`1 2 3 ♯4 5 (♯5) 6 7 1` |
| C7 | `C D E F G A B♭ (B) C`<br>`1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7 (7) 1` |
| Cm7 | `C D E♭ F G A B♭ (B) C`<br>`1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7 (7) 1` |

In these scales notes often sit right next to each other. The app was tested on that: it keeps up
when you play about two notes a second or slower. The details are in
[the judge's measurements](judge-measurements.md), under *Lines in order*.

## The switches

| switch | what it does |
|---|---|
| **Single notes** | You play one note at a time. Off: you may strike the whole grip at once |
| **In order** | You play a set in the order on screen. Off: in any order. Every other exercise always goes in order |
| **Struck only** | Only a note you really struck counts, not one still ringing from before |
| **Random order** | Mixes up the notes of the sets and of the first exercise in each structure, separately for every chord |
| **Shuffle the chords as well** | Mixes up the order of the chords in the song too |
| **Voice leading** | With the shuffle on, starts every chord on the note nearest the one you played last |
| **View** | Shows the numbers, the grip on the neck, or tiles with the note name under every number |
| **Chord strip** | Shows the chords of the song at the top of the window. Off: you keep them in your head |

## Where to start

1. **One tone**, then **Two tones: 3 7**. Play through a whole song, slowly. Visualise those two notes
   on the neck and listen to how they move.
2. **Three** and **Four tones**, then their inversions: the same chord started on different notes.
3. **Structure 1–5** and **5–9** with the shuffle on.
4. **Voice leading** in a song with a II–V–I in it, like Autumn Leaves.
5. **Chord scale** from `3` and from `7`, and last the **Passing-note scale**.
6. Any of these again [without the chord strip](#playing-without-the-chord-strip).
