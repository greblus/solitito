# Practising intervals

A guide to the fifty exercises of Intervals: what each one asks you to play, how it changes
from chord to chord, and how to read the list they are chosen from. Every example below was
printed by the app itself, so what you read here is what the judge waits for.

## What an exercise is

An exercise is written once, as degrees, and read against each chord of the tune as it comes.
`3` is not a note — it is *the third of the chord on screen*: E over C maj7, E♭ over Cm7. That
is what lets one exercise run through a whole standard, and what makes it ear training rather
than a fingering: the shape on the neck changes from chord to chord, the sound of "the third"
does not.

`1`, `3`, `5` and `7` are always the chord's own tones, however the chord spells them — the
fifth of Cm7♭5 is G♭, the seventh of C°7 is B𝄫 (A). Anything else — `2`, `4`, `6`, `9` — comes
from the chord's scale:

| chord | scale | over C |
|---|---|---|
| maj7 | Lydian | C D E F♯ G A B — but a suspension's `4` is the plain F |
| 7 | Mixolydian | C D E F G A B♭ |
| m7 | Dorian | C D E♭ F G A B♭ |
| m7♭5 | Locrian ♮9 | C D E♭ F G♭ A♭ B♭ |
| °7 | whole-half diminished | C D E♭ F G♭ A♭ B𝄫 (A) |
| m6, m(maj7) | melodic minor | C D E♭ F G A B |

A tone above the octave carries its compound number: `8` is the root an octave up, then `9`,
`10`, `12`, `14`. A tone below the root is written plain — `7` at the end of `3 2 1 7` is the
seventh *under* the root.

## Reading the list

The list names each exercise by its group and its tones over a major or dominant chord. Where a
minor or half-diminished chord takes other tones, they follow `m:`; where a diminished chord
takes others again, they follow `°:`:

```
Structure 1–5: 1 2 3 5 · m: 1 3 4 5
Structure 5–9: 6 5 9 7 · m: 7 5 9 8 · °: b6 5 9 7
```

The numbers after `m:` are degrees of *that* chord, so the `3` there is its minor third — over
Cm7 the first line is C E♭ F G. A minor chord trades the second for the fourth: a second lies a
semitone from the minor third and clashes with it.

`↻` marks a random inversion, drawn again for every chord: `1 3 5 ↻` may come as `3 5 8` over
one chord and `5 8 10` over the next.

Voice leading is numbered instead of spelled out: its cells differ from chord to chord, so a
single line of degrees would say less than the number.

## The groups, with examples

The examples are over C, so the chord's own spelling shows: C maj7, C7, Cm7, Cm7♭5, C°7.

### One, two and three tones

Single chord tones, pairs and triads — `1`, `3`, `5`, `7`, `1 3`, `1 5`, `1 7`, `3 5`, `3 7`,
`1 3 5`, `1 3 7`, `3 5 7`, and `1 3 5 ↻`. These are **sets**: with **In order** off they may be
played in any order, with it on in the order shown.

| exercise | C maj7 | C7 | Cm7 | Cm7♭5 | C°7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `3` | E | E | E♭ | E♭ | E♭ |
| `3 7` | E B | E B♭ | E♭ B♭ | E♭ B♭ | E♭ A |
| `1 3 5` | C E G | C E G | C E♭ G | C E♭ G♭ | C E♭ G♭ |
| `3 5 7` | E G B | E G B♭ | E♭ G B♭ | E♭ G♭ B♭ | E♭ G♭ A |

`3 7` is the shell of the chord — the two tones that say what it is. Played through a tune it is
the line jazz players hear moving underneath the changes.

### Four tones, and four tones to the octave

The seventh chord, then its inversions written as lines climbing past the octave:

| exercise | C maj7 | Cm7 | C°7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| `1 3 5 7` | C E G B | C E♭ G B♭ | C E♭ G♭ A |
| `3 5 7 8` | E G B C | E♭ G B♭ C | E♭ G♭ A C |
| `5 7 8 10` | G B C E | G B♭ C E♭ | G♭ A C E♭ |
| `7 8 10 12` | B C E G | B♭ C E♭ G | A C E♭ G♭ |

**Four tones to the octave** add the starting tone again on top — `1 3 5 7 8`, `3 5 7 8 10`,
`5 7 8 10 12`, `7 8 10 12 14` — so each one is a complete arpeggio from that tone to its octave.
Only `1 3 5 7` is a set; the inversions are lines, played in their order.

### Structure 1–5 and Structure 5–9

Four notes of the chord's pentatonic sound, in four orders and turned (`↻`). From the root:

| exercise | C maj7, C7 | Cm7 | Cm7♭5 | C°7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `1 2 3 5` | C D E G | C E♭ F G | C E♭ F G♭ | C D E♭ G♭ |
| `2 1 5 3` | D C G E | E♭ C G F | E♭ C G♭ F | D C G♭ E♭ |
| `3 5 2 1` | E G D C | F G E♭ C | F G♭ E♭ C | E♭ G♭ D C |
| `5 1 2 3` | G C D E | G C E♭ F | G♭ C E♭ F | G♭ C D E♭ |

From the fifth, the same shapes — the hand plays what it already knows, and the ear hears the
sixth, the seventh and the ninth:

| exercise | C maj7 | C7 | Cm7 | C°7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `5 6 7 9` | G A B D | G A B♭ D | G B♭ C D | G♭ A♭ A D |
| `6 5 9 7` | A G D B | A G D B♭ | B♭ G D C | A♭ G♭ D A |

The first cell of each structure is the one the shuffle deals in other orders; the three after
it are its other orders already written out, so they always come as written.

### Voice leading

Lines through II–V–I: each chord's cell ends a step from where the next one starts, most often
the seventh falling to the next chord's third. Over Cm7 – F7 – B♭maj7, the opening of Autumn
Leaves:

| exercise | Cm7 | F7 | B♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voice leading 1 | E♭ D C B♭ | A G F E♭ | D C B♭ A |
| Voice leading 2 | E♭ D C B♭ | A C E♭ G | F B♭ C D |
| Voice leading 3 | C E♭ G B♭ | A G F E♭ | D C B♭ A |
| Voice leading 4 | C E♭ G B♭ | A C E♭ G | D C B♭ A |
| Voice leading 5 | G E♭ C B♭ | A C E♭ G | D C B♭ A |

Follow the joins: B♭ at the end of Cm7 falls a semitone to A, the third of F7; E♭ or G at the
end of F7 moves a step to the D or F that B♭maj7 starts on. Played in tempo, these are bebop
lines, not exercises — which is the point. They only make sense over the progression as
written, so the shuffle leaves their order alone.

### Suspension resolved

`2 3 1` and `4 3 1`: a suspended tone falling to the third, then the root. Over B♭maj7 the `4`
is the plain E♭ — a suspension, not the Lydian ♯4:

| exercise | Cm7 | F7 | B♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| `2 3 1` | D E♭ C | G A F | C D B♭ |
| `4 3 1` | F E♭ C | B♭ A F | E♭ D B♭ |

### Chord scale, and with the passing note

The chord's scale through an octave, from the root, the third, the fifth, the seventh, a chord
tone drawn for each chord, or any tone of the scale drawn for each chord. From the third:

| chord | Chord scale: 3 |
|---|---|
| C maj7 | E F♯ G A B C D E |
| C7 | E F G A B♭ C D E |
| Cm7 | E♭ F G A B♭ C D E♭ |
| Cm7♭5 | E♭ F G♭ A♭ B♭ C D E♭ |

**With the passing note** adds one chromatic tone — `(♯5)` over maj7, `(7)` over the others — so
that eight notes fill the octave and, played in eighths from a downbeat, every chord tone falls
on a beat:

| chord | Passing-note scale: 1 |
|---|---|
| C maj7 | C D E F♯ G (G♯) A B C |
| C7 | C D E F G A B♭ (B) C |
| Cm7 | C D E♭ F G A B♭ (B) C |

The semitone steps these scales are full of were measured before these exercises went in: no
step is credited early off the note before it, and the judge keeps up from about half a second
a note — see *Lines in order* in [the judge's measurements](judge-measurements.md).

## The options that change an exercise

| option | what it does here |
|---|---|
| **Single notes** | One note at a time. Off, a strummed grip passes its tones at once |
| **In order** | Sets in the order shown. Off, a set may be played in any order. Lines are always in order |
| **Struck only** | A note counts only where it was struck, not off one still ringing |
| **Random order** | Deals a set, or the first cell of a structure, in another order for each chord |
| **Shuffle the chords as well** | With the shuffle, the chords of the tune come in a drawn order too |
| **Voice leading** | With the shuffle, each chord starts on its tone nearest the last note played |
| **View** | Degree names, the grip or line on the neck, or tiles with the note each degree falls on |

## A practice order that works

1. **One tone**, then **Two tones: 3 7**, through a whole standard, slowly. Hear the shell move.
2. **Three** and **Four tones**, then their inversions — the same chord from every tone.
3. **Structure 1–5** and **5–9** with the shuffle on: the shapes stop being shapes.
4. **Voice leading** over a tune with II–V–I in it, in tempo.
5. **Chord scale** from the third and the seventh, then **with the passing note** in eighths.
