# Practising with formulas

A step-by-step guide to the three formula exercises: what to play, what to listen for, and
what every row on the screen is telling you while you play it.

[← back to the README](../README.md)

---

## What a formula is

A formula is a set of intervals over a root, written as functions rather than notes:

```
1 b3 5 b7
```

That is all it is. Not a scale — there is no fixed order to walk. Not a chord — there is no
obligation to sound them together. Five or six of the twelve chromatic functions, chosen at
random, that you have to find on the neck and play in any order you like.

Two things follow, and they are the whole point of the mode:

- **It has no name.** `1 b2 3 b5 6` is not "the something-something scale", so there is no
  fingering to recall and no pattern to run up and down. You have to hear where each function
  is and find it. That is a different skill from playing scales, and it is the one improvising
  actually uses.
- **It is the same exercise in every key.** The functions do not move; only where they land
  does. A formula practised properly ends up under your fingers everywhere, because it was
  never learned as a shape in one place.

There are 2048 of them — every subset of the twelve chromatic functions that contains the
root.

## The screen, row by row

Over a chord, the column reads top to bottom as one sentence. Here is the fullest version,
with everything switched on:

```
              Autumn Leaves          the tune, in "Over the changes" only
                   F7                the chord you are playing over
             Placed from VII         where the formula's 1 sits on that chord
              Relative to F          heading for the row under it
          7    b3    3    4   b6     the formula's notes, named from the CHORD
          1    3     4   b5    6     THE FORMULA — this is what you play
          E    G#    A   Bb   C#     the letters, if you want them
      colours it · 1 of 4 chord tones
      Nearest scale: Lydian dominant
        1  2  3  b5  5  6  b7        its degrees, yours picked out in blue
      Chords inside the formula:
          bVm7     bVdim
          Em7      C#dim
                   ★                 keep this formula under a name
                your turn            shown while paused
                   ⏸
       next chord      another formula
```

**The big row is the exercise.** Everything else is there to explain it, and every part of it
can be switched off in Practice settings once it has done its job.

- **The functions**, large, in the middle. They light **green** one by one as the app hears
  you play them. When the last one turns green the lap is over: a short pause, and the same
  formula comes round again.
- **Relative to F** and the small row above the functions — only over a chord. The same
  notes, counted from the chord's root instead of the formula's. The ones in **blue** are the
  chord's own tones. This row is the lesson in the harmony exercises; more on it below.
- **The letters** under the functions. Useful when checking yourself on the neck, and the
  first thing to switch off: reading letters is the habit the mode exists to break.
- **The verdict line** — `colours it · 1 of 4 chord tones` — counts how much of the chord the
  formula covers. Again, harmony exercises only.
- **Nearest scale** is the bridge from what you already know. `1 2 3 b5 5 6 b7` with your
  functions picked out says "this is Lydian dominant, minus the 4" — which is often the
  fastest way into a set that looks alien.
- **Chords inside the formula** are chords whose every note is in the set, so you can play one
  without stepping outside the formula. They are smaller than the set, not equal to it: a
  triad uses three of your five notes and a seventh chord four, so each is a piece of the
  formula rather than the whole of it. Two things they are good for — a shape you already know
  to grab hold of inside a set you do not, and a way of playing the formula as harmony rather
  than one note at a time. Only the fullest are listed: a chord wholly contained in another on
  the row would say nothing new. Over a chord they are numbered from that chord, so `bVm7`
  means "a minor seventh built on the flat fifth of the chord you are on".
- **The star** keeps the formula under a name. Named formulas live in a list in Practice
  settings and can be drawn again any time; a cross on a row in that list throws one out. A
  formula is stored without a key, because a formula is key-independent — the same exercise
  would otherwise appear twelve times. Use it for sets you liked the sound of, not as a list
  to work through: the point is exploring, not memorising.
- **Pause** is not a halt. The whole set turns blue, nothing is judged, and the line says
  *your turn*: improvise inside the formula for as long as you like. This is where the mode
  turns from an exercise into playing.

## Set up a click and a recorder first

Solitito has no metronome and does not record — it only listens and judges. Both jobs belong
to something else, and doing them is not optional if you want this to work:

- **Play to a click.** Any DAW will give you one, as will a phone app. Without it a hunted
  note simply takes as long as it takes, and the hunting never gets shorter. With it you find
  out what you can actually play in time, which is a different and much smaller set than what
  you can play.
- **Record every session**, and record it with the click running — every DAW does this out of
  the box, and it costs one keystroke. Do not record only the good takes; the useful ones are
  the ordinary ones.
- **Listen back afterwards.** This is the part people skip and it is the part that teaches.
  Playing and judging cannot happen at the same time — while your hands are busy you hear
  what you meant, and on playback you hear what came out. The gap between those two is the
  entire lesson, and it is invisible from inside the take.

A useful trick when listening back: play a simple groove or a chord against your own
recording — the standing chord for the harmony exercises, or just the root — and hear what
your lines did against it. What sounded shapeless while playing often turns out to have
been perfectly clear, and the reverse happens just as often.

## Exercise 1 — Formula in a key

**What it is.** A drawn formula over a drawn root. No chord, no tune. This is the mode as it
has always been, and it is where to start.

<img height="440" alt="Formulas, a formula in a key" src="solitito_formulas.png" />
</div>

**How to practise it.**

1. Set **Notes in a formula** to 4 or 5. Not because bigger sets are too hard, but because
   they stop working: a scale box holds seven notes, so a seven-note formula is close enough
   to something your hands already know that they will fall into the rut on their own. Four
   or five notes leave no rut to fall into — you have to look for every one of them. Any
   length is worth practising later; this is where the looking is sharpest. Five minutes of
   it done with control is worth more than twenty done on autopilot — build up from there.
2. Read the functions and find the first one on the neck. Play it. It goes green.
3. Find the rest, in any order, anywhere on the neck. Take your time — a note has to sound
   clearly to be credited, and hunting for it silently is part of the work.
4. When the set completes, the same formula comes back. **Play it again somewhere else** —
   another position, another string set, another octave.
5. After three or four laps, hit **pause** and improvise inside the set for a minute. Then
   press it again and carry on.

**What to look at.** At the beginning, everything: letters, nearest scale, chords inside.
Once the same formula stops feeling foreign, switch the letters off and work from functions
alone. That is the moment the mode starts doing its job.

**Settings that shape this exercise.**

<div align="center">
<img width="330" alt="Practice settings for Formulas" src="solitito_settings8.png" />
</div>

| setting | what it is for |
|---|---|
| **Notes in a formula** | 4–5 for a good start — too few to fall back on a scale shape. Any length is an exercise worth doing later |
| **Key** | Fix it to work one key for a while, or leave it drawn per formula |
| **Must contain** | `b3 b7` to only draw minor-seventh-flavoured sets, `b5` for tension, and so on |
| **Play the notes in order** | Turns the set into a line, lowest function first. Harder, and good for hearing the shape rather than hunting the easy notes first |
| **Favourites** | Star a set you want to come back to tomorrow |

**Nothing here is to be memorised.** The star is not a syllabus — it is for sets whose sound
you liked and want to meet again, not a list to get through. Do not get attached to a key, a
position or a formula; keep it moving. A formula you can play from memory has quietly turned
back into a pattern, which is the thing the mode exists to get away from.

**A drill worth doing.** Fix the key to C, set five notes, and play whatever comes up.
Star the two or three that sounded like something. Tomorrow, draw fresh ones — and if one of
the starred sets still sounds good on the second meeting, that one has earned its place.

## Exercise 2 — Over a chord

**What it is.** One chord, drawn at random and standing still, with the formula planted on one
of its twelve degrees. This is where the mode stops being about the formula alone and starts
being about harmony.

**The idea, in one sentence.** The same formula sounds like a completely different thing
depending on *which degree of the chord you plant its root on* — and how different is not a
matter of taste, it is a count of how many of the chord's tones the formula covers.

Take the plainest formula there is, a major triad — `1 3 5` — and put it on `C7` from four
different degrees:

| planted on | you play | what those notes are to C7 |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | C E G | `1 3 5` — the chord, minus its seventh |
| **bVII** | Bb D F | `b7 9 11` — the seventh plus two tensions, open and quartal |
| **II** | D F# A | `9 #11 13` — not one note of the chord: pure colour |
| **bVI** | Ab C Eb | `b13 1 #9` — the altered sound |

One formula, three notes long, and four completely different results. Nothing changed but
where you started it. Note the third row especially: a D major triad over C7 shares no note
at all with the chord, and it is one of the most usable sounds on the list — which is the
best argument there is against reading *outside* as *wrong*.

### Which placement counts as what

One rule decides it, and it is applied to all twelve degrees. The app takes your formula
planted on a degree, sees which of the chord's notes it covers, and asks about the chord's
**characteristic tones** — every tone of the chord except its root and its perfect fifth,
because only those say which chord it is. A minor seventh, a dominant and a major seventh all
have a root and a fifth; what tells them apart is the third and the seventh (and the flat
fifth of a `m7b5`, which is characteristic too — it is not a perfect fifth).

- **spells the chord out** — the placement covers every characteristic tone
- **outside it** — it covers no tone of the chord at all
- **colours it** — everything in between

Worked through for one screen: `AbMaj7` (Ab C Eb G, so C and G are the characteristic tones)
with the formula `1 b3 4 5 7`.

| planted on | root lands on | you play | chord tones hit | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | Ab | Ab B Db Eb G | 3 | colours |
| bII | A | A C D E Ab | 2 | colours |
| II | Bb | Bb Db Eb F A | 1 | colours |
| bIII | B | B D E Gb Bb | 0 | **outside** |
| III | C | C Eb F G B | 3 | **spells it out** |
| IV | Db | Db E Gb Ab C | 2 | colours |
| bV | D | D F G A Db | 1 | colours |
| V | Eb | Eb Gb Ab Bb D | 2 | colours |
| bVI | E | E G A B Eb | 2 | colours |
| VI | F | F Ab Bb C E | 2 | colours |
| bVII | Gb | Gb A B Db F | 0 | **outside** |
| VII | G | G Bb C D Gb | 2 | **spells it out** |

So with **Placement** set to *spells the chord out*, this formula over this chord leaves the
draw exactly two degrees to choose between. The kind you ask for is drawn at random from the
degrees that qualify — and if none qualifies, which happens, you get one of the twelve rather
than nothing.

Look at the first row, because it explains why this is not a matter of counting. Planted on
the chord's own root, the formula hits **three** of the four chord tones and still only
colours it: its `b3` is B — that is Cb, not the C the chord needs to be major. Three hits
without the third say less about the chord than two hits that include the third and the
seventh.

**How to practise it.**

1. Set **Placement** to *spells the chord out*. Play a few laps. This is the safe end — the
   formula carries the chord's third and seventh, and it sounds like the chord.
2. Switch to *colours it* and play the same formula again. Listen for what changed. The blue
   row tells you exactly what: fewer blue notes, more colour.
3. Switch to *outside it*. Now nothing you play belongs to the chord. It will sound wrong
   until it does not — and learning to hear that as tension rather than as a mistake is the
   whole reason the setting exists.
4. Then set it to *any* and let the app draw. Before you look at the verdict line, guess:
   does this one spell the chord out, colour it, or sit outside?

That last step is the exercise. The screen is the answer key, so use it as one: play first,
guess, then read.

**What the rows are doing for you.**

- **Relative to F** (the small row above the functions) is the translation you are trying to
  learn to do in your head. `b5` in the big row and `4` above it means: *this note is the flat
  fifth of my formula and the eleventh of the chord under it*.
- **Blue** marks the notes that belong to the chord. Two blues under a five-note formula means
  three of your notes are colour — that is the sound you are hearing.
- **The verdict**, `colours it · 1 of 4 chord tones`, counts it for you. *Spells it out*
  means the formula carries what fixes the chord's quality: its third and seventh, and the
  flat fifth of a `m7b5`. The root and the fifth do not count towards it — they say nothing
  about which chord it is.
- **Chords inside the formula**, numbered from the chord, is the "what can I superimpose here"
  row. If it says `IIm` while you are on a dominant chord, you have a minor triad from the
  ninth sitting inside your formula, ready to be played as a unit.
- **Jazz names** (a setting) rewrites the small row the way a chart would: what belongs to the
  chord keeps the chord's own function, and what does not becomes `b9 9 #9 11 #11 b13 13`.
  Turn it on when `b6` starts reading as "a note the chord doesn't have" rather than as a
  chord tone that is missing.

**Where the lap goes.** Finishing the set draws **a new placement on the same chord**. That is
deliberate: this exercise is one formula heard from twelve places, and the chord has to stand
still for that to happen. A new chord comes from *another formula*.

## Exercise 3 — Over the changes

**What it is.** The same formula carried across a whole standard, replanted on every chord.
The tune comes from the same library Chords reads from, so your own `user_songs.txt` works
here too.

Four things make the difference between practising this and flailing at it:

- **Use smaller formulas when the changes move.** On a tune that turns over every two beats
  there is no time to find seven notes, and a four-note set played musically beats a
  seven-note one played in a panic.
- **Decide before you start** what you want over each chord: its own sound, part of it, or
  something planted from another degree. Choosing in the moment is a later skill.
- **Connect one placement to the next by the smallest interval you can.** When the chord
  changes, do not jump to the new root because it is the root — find the nearest note of the
  new set to where your hand already is. That is what makes it sound joined up.
- **If it is too hard, make it easier and keep playing.** Ignore some of the changes, give
  each chord twice as long, or slow the tune down. Music being made inside a limit is the
  point; speed is not.

**How to practise it.**

1. Pick a tune and a formula. Leave **Placement** on *any* the first few times through.
2. Play the set over the first chord. When it completes, the app moves to the next chord of
   the tune and draws a placement on it — the formula does not change.
3. Use **next chord** under the pause button to move on without finishing the set: the point
   is playing over the changes, not completing a checklist.
4. Use **another formula** when you want a different set to carry through the tune.
5. Do a whole chorus, then pause and improvise over the standing chord for a while.
6. Record the chorus with the click running and listen back before you play another. Over
   changes especially, the joins between chords sound very different from the outside.

**A harder version.** Confine yourself to four frets. Play the whole
tune without leaving that zone, then move to another zone and do it again. The app does not
enforce it — but the fretboard trainer's region line (`E A D · 5-8`) is a good way to pick a
zone and stick to it.

**What to watch here.** The tune's name and the chord are at the top, so you always know where
you are. The small row and the verdict change on every chord, and after a few choruses you
will start predicting them — that is the skill arriving.

## A practice order that works

| stage | exercise | settings | what you are learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formula in a key | 4 notes, letters on | to find functions on the neck at all |
| 2 | Formula in a key | 5 notes, letters off | to hear a set instead of reading it |
| 3 | Over a chord | placement: *spells the chord out* | that a formula can be the chord |
| 4 | Over a chord | *colours it*, then *outside it* | what colour and tension sound like |
| 5 | Over a chord | *any*, guess before reading | to hear which of the three it is |
| 6 | Over the changes | small formulas, slow tune | to carry one set through harmony |

None of this needs to be finished before the next one starts. Ten minutes of stage 1 as a
warm-up is worth as much on the day you begin as it is a month later.

## Things worth knowing while you play

- **A note has to be heard to count.** Play it cleanly and let it ring. A note fretted but
  barely sounded will not be credited, and that is not the app being difficult — it is the
  same standard your ear applies.
- **Nothing is credited twice.** Once a function is green it stays green for the lap; playing
  it again does nothing. If a function refuses to light, it is usually the neighbouring
  semitone being heard instead — check the letters row.
- **Pause is a real part of the exercise**, not a break from it. The mode teaches sets; the
  pause is where you find out what you can do with one.
- **Console debug** (Practice settings) prints a line for every credited function with what
  the app heard, if you want to see the judging rather than argue with it.
